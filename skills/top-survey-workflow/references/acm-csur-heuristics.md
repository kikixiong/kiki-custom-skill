# ACM Computing Surveys Heuristics

Use this as an editor-and-reviewer lens, not as a formal venue policy.

## What Usually Helps

- The survey covers an area that is clearly active, consequential, and confusing enough to need synthesis.
- The paper has a memorable organizing frame: taxonomy, pipeline, stack, design space, or debate map.
- The paper explains why prior surveys are no longer enough.
- The paper is broad, but the scope boundary is explicit and defensible.
- Comparative tables and overview figures carry real analytical value.
- The survey is critical and synthetic, not a sequence of isolated paper summaries.
- Open problems are concrete and research-shaping rather than generic.

## What Often Gets Punished

- Topic is too broad, causing superficial coverage.
- Topic is too narrow, making the paper feel like an extended tutorial or niche bibliography.
- No novel organizing principle.
- Too much description, not enough comparison.
- Weak differentiation from existing surveys.
- Outdated literature pool or poor recent coverage.
- Claims of comprehensiveness without transparent inclusion logic.
- Future directions that read like boilerplate.

## Strong Internal Questions

Ask these before submission:

1. If a reader only keeps one thing from this survey, what is it?
2. What is the one figure or table that justifies the paper's existence?
3. Which existing surveys would a reviewer compare this against, and why does this one still need to exist?
4. If ten landmark papers are added tomorrow, does the taxonomy still hold?
5. Does each main section contain at least one nontrivial synthesis or tension?
6. Would a senior PhD student use this paper to enter the field efficiently?

## Minimal Deliverables Before Submission

- prior-survey differentiation paragraph in the introduction
- explicit corpus construction note
- stable taxonomy figure
- representative comparison table
- reviewer-style internal critique
- line-edited abstract and conclusion
